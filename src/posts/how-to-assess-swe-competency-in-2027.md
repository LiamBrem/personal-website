### aka what makes a good interview

Being in college and around people recruiting for newgrad/intern SWE roles, I’ve been hearing a lot of discussion about the state of SWE interviews. In particular, since the introduction of AI-assisted interviews, across the industry, interview formats have become much less standardized.



I’ve personally been thinking a lot about what makes a *good* SWE interview, or if you break it down, how can you reasonably interview candidates and filter for the ones that would perform the best as a SWE.



The biggest challenge for interviews in the tech industry is how impossible it is to test for specific skills.



However, domain knowledge/specific skillsets never usually translated one-to-one with who performs best as an Engineer; sure, there’s a strong correlation between the two, but oftentimes one’s problem-solving ability matters much more than if they know a specific language feature that may become outdated in 5 years.



That’s why leetcode-style interviews became so widespread in the first place. They are a solid means of testing a candidate’s problem solving ability without requiring any specific knowledge outside of 1 programming language (and DSA of course).



Contrary to what I see so many people saying, I think these interviews are a good way to test candidates for the above reasons. They’re by no means perfect, which is why so many people are eager to find something better and why I’m writing this in the first place.



If we think about what makes a successful engineer (or a good hire), it mainly comes down to someone’s ability to learn quickly, reason through difficult problems and come up with a solution.



I recently went through Palantir’s “Learning” interview round and, after reflecting on it, think it’s one of the most optimal interviews.   
  
How it works is simple: the interviewer teaches the interviewee about a topic that they haven’t seen before (e.g. a specific data structure, protocol, or library) for a few minutes. After the interviewee confirms their understanding through a few follow-up questions, they then go through several increasingly challenging rounds solving short coding tasks using and/or implementing this new topic.



The benefit that this style has over the leetcode-esque question is that there aren’t any ‘gotcha’ moments. So many of these problems revolve around a single ‘trick’ that isn’t the most indicative of one’s problem-solving ability. It also reduces the advantage of having previously encountered a particular problem or its underlying solution.



This also gives the interviewer much more insight into how a candidate thinks. What questions do they ask when introduced to something new? How quickly can they develop an understanding of it? Can they take what they’ve just learned and expand beyond what they were explicitly taught?



I’m surprised I haven’t seen this type of interview in more places, particularly given the direction the industry is heading. As AI continues to lower the barrier to implementing software, I think the ability to reason through unfamiliar problems will become increasingly valuable. Rather than trying to reinvent technical interviews around the introduction of AI, perhaps we should be rethinking what we’re trying to measure in the first place.
