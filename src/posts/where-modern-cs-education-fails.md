## Background



I’m in my 3rd and final year of my Computer Science education at a large state school. Prior to starting, I had really high expectations for the standard of my education. Sure, it’s not Stanford or MIT, but it’s a large school with tens-of-thousands of students, loads of funding, and is ranked top 50 for CS.



After a couple years, I can confidently say that the quality of my education came nowhere close to my original expectations. While I am obviously a small sample size, I have talked with other people who go to similarly sized/similarly ranked schools, and my experience doesn’t seem to be unique.



There are many differing opinions on what the goal of an undergraduate Computer Science degree is. Many people argue that it should prepare students entirely for industry; others claim that schools should be more focused on research. The problem is that these, and a bunch of other viewpoints, are correct, which makes it difficult to design a one-size-fits-all system. Nonetheless, something that I think everyone can agree on is that a *vast* majority of students attending one of these institutions are there to eventually end up in industry (most likely SWE). This is something that schools do recognize, so that is going to be the primary emphasis of this writing.



So, why exactly am I unsatisfied with this (rather expensive) education? I’ve summarized it into **5** main reasons:



# What Modern CS Education is Getting Wrong



## 1. Fundamental concepts are being pushed to the side to make room for “trendier” subjects.



My school dropped Operating Systems as a requirement to replace it with a course covering probability and statistics (after statistics was already previously a requirement). My school went an entire academic year without offering a compilers course. AI is taking over the industry and changing what the field looks like every few weeks. However, what doesn’t change are the fundamentals that have been around for decades. Schools *should* expose students to AI tooling/workflows/development etc. even [CMU has an agentic ai course they’re making publicly available](https://www.cmu-agents.com/). But, de-emphasizing the fundamentals is only doing the students a disservice.



## 2. The people designing CS curricula are often disconnected from the environments graduates enter.



My school has a board of mostly alumni who work in industry, and their job is to specifically to advise the school administration on how to develop the curricula based on current trends, growing skillsets, etc. This sounds great! The problem? Most people on this board have been in the industry for several decades. Having experienced engineers and executives involved in curriculum development is undoubtedly valuable. However, someone who has spent the last 20 years climbing the corporate ladder may have a very different perspective on what it takes to succeed as an entry-level engineer today.



The skills required to be an effective engineering manager, director, or CTO aren’t necessarily the same skills required of a new graduate joining a software engineering team. And while these individuals might have a great understanding of where the industry is headed, they’re often far removed from the day-to-day challenges faced by junior engineers.



## 3. The curriculum is designed around the average student, potentially underserving both ends.



One of the biggest challenges universities face is that students enter their CS programs with drastically different levels of experience. Some have been programming since middle school, while others have never written a line of code in their lives. Despite this, almost everyone is expected to follow essentially the same curriculum, at essentially the same pace.



For students with prior experience, this can mean spending entire semesters reviewing concepts they’ve already mastered. For complete beginners, it can mean struggling to keep up as courses move on to new material before they’ve fully grasped the fundamentals. Neither group is being particularly well served.



Of course, universities need some amount of standardization, but there’s a significant difference between providing structure and forcing everyone through an almost identical educational experience.



## 4. Universities teach students to build things from scratch, but rarely to understand things that already exist.



In almost every project in every single CS course I took outside OS, I had to start with a blank file or maybe a few files containing less than 100 lines of code. Throughout my internships, I almost never started a new project/feature in complete isolation. I had to become good at reading through code and features, build up a mental model of how things worked, and then write code that interacted with it.   
  
Especially now with AI, it’s becoming even more important of a skill to be able to understand and integrate with existing systems. This is something that most graduates just don’t posses.



## 5. Universities don’t place enough emphasis on building something meaningful from scratch.



This may sound like the opposite of the previous point, but there’s an important nuance.



Paul Graham recently wrote [an essay](https://www.paulgraham.com/prepare.html) titled “How Universities Should Prepare Founders.” While being a founder takes an entirely different skillset & personality than becoming an engineer, many of his arguments apply just as well to students pursuing careers in software engineering.



One point that really resonated with me was his emphasis on encouraging students to work on their own projects.



There’s a fundamental difference between completing a programming assignment with a pre-identified problem, scope, and requirements and creating something entirely on your own.



With a personal project, none of that work has been done for you. You have to identify a problem worth solving, decide what to build, figure out which technologies to use, possible learn how to use those technologies, and work through challenges that you may not even know exist yet. Unlike a course assignment, there isn’t necessarily a point where you’ve satisfied all the requirements and can call it finished.



I also think there’s something to be said about the motivation behind these projects. When you’re building something because you genuinely want it to exist, you’re much more likely to go deep and learn things well beyond what’s required to pass a class.



### Common complaints about university education that I generally agree with:



**“There are too many gen-eds.”**  
Each semester, the technical courses I was taking made up only about *half* of my total course load. I strongly dislike the argument that this makes students “well-rounded.”



**“The professors aren’t good teachers.”**I’d argue that the majority of the professors I had were excellent educators who had students’ best interests in mind. That being said, there are definitely some that are distinctly lacking the ability to teach effectively.



**“The system incentivizes completion rather than curiosity.”**This one is pretty self explanatory; I’ve never really seen an effort to push students to explore things outside of class content.



**“The teaching methodologies are outdated.”**One example of this is from some early programming classes at my school (ex. DSA): if the end goal is to get students better at programming and understanding the underlying concepts, the most effective way to do that is by having them write more code. If this is the case, why is there only one 50-100 line assignment do every several weeks?



# What Modern CS Education is Getting Right



## 1. Universities teach students how to think rigorously and solve problems.



One thing I think universities do particularly well is emphasize *why* something works, rather than simply teaching students how to make it work. Courses like discrete math, algorithms, and computer architecture force students to reason through difficult problems and understand the underlying principles. This speaks more broadly to problem solving in general; these skills are far more valuable in the long run than knowing any particular programming language or framework.



## 2. School exposes students to areas they wouldn’t otherwise explore.



If I were learning computer science entirely on my own, I probably wouldn’t have voluntarily spent much time studying topics like operating systems, computer architecture, or formal methods. A structured curriculum forces students to explore areas they might otherwise overlook, potentially introducing them to interests they never knew they had. Even when those subjects don’t end up being directly relevant to their careers, they contribute to a broader understanding of computing.



## 3. Universities provide a structured progression of increasingly difficult material.



One of the hardest parts of learning something independently is knowing what to learn next. Universities largely solve this problem by organizing courses so that each builds upon knowledge acquired in previous ones. Rather than jumping between disconnected tutorials and projects, students (especially in the beginning of their degree) follow a relatively coherent path from fundamental programming concepts to more advanced topics.



## 4. School creates an environment where students learn from each other.



Some of the most valuable things I’ve learned in college haven’t come from professors or lectures, but from other students. Being surrounded by people with similar interests, but different experiences and perspectives, creates countless opportunities to grow and learn. This is something that’s particularly difficult to replicate through self-directed or online education.

## Looking Forward



I don’t regret my degree at all; I’ve had many amazing professors and got to meet many amazing people along the way. However, I think there is plenty of room to be improved. It’s not my goal for this to come across as one large complaint; rather, I’m optimistic that universities will start to recognize the importance of some of the things I’ve talked about and act on it for the benefit of everyone.
